## Are We Still Needed?

We all feel it. Every other week, we lose or delegate another part of our work to agents. It started with giving up on writing code. Then it expanded to pretty much any writing - Slack messages, documents, emails. Since around Opus 4.6, debugging, once an art and something that made us proud, became clicking on the recommended option in Cursor. And with better harnesses, even architecture and high-level thinking started moving to agents.

My day-to-day now mostly consists of making sure my agents aren't blocking other people's agents (which is another topic worth writing about).

This naturally leads us to think that this is it. We, engineers, are doomed.

But before you start thinking, again, about which jobs are "AI-protected", let's take a step back.

We still have some *real* leverage over agents, and let me try to prove it using my favorite mathematical trick - [*reductio ad absurdum*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductio_ad_absurdum), aka "proof by contradiction".

1. Assume we have no real leverage over agents. The agents are doing all the thinking, and we're just getting in their way.
2. We all have access to roughly the same models and harnesses.
3. So, if the human doesn't matter, the difference between engineers' output should shrink dramatically.
4. But that's not what we're seeing. Great engineers are still producing much better results than average engineers. If anything, 10x engineers have become 100x engineers. And the average engineer is still average.
5. Therefore, there's still *something* the human brings to the table.

Now here's the thing about proof by contradiction - it can tell you that an assumption is wrong, but it doesn't tell you why.

## So What Is It?

Now that we've established your trust that humans still have some leverage, let's try to understand what it actually is.

Let's reverse-engineer it from a few observations:

1. Sometimes agents get lost when the problem isn't defined well enough. As a human, I tend to keep asking questions until I'm sure we're solving the actual problem.
2. Sometimes agents overcomplicate simple things or oversimplify complex ones. I have some intuition about which problems are genuinely hard and which ones can be kept simple.
3. Agents don't really feel ownership. They optimize for solving the problem in front of them, not necessarily for maintaining the solution over the next two years.
4. And sometimes there's just something I can't quite put my finger on. I know something is wrong, but can't really explain why.

A cool example of that last point is our allergy to AI slop. We all know that feeling when we read less than a single sentence and immediately *know* that an AI wrote it, so we just stop reading. We don't necessarily know why, but something feels off.

We've even developed a whole language around it - slop, slop canon, slop grande, and an entire meme culture. Somehow, we've developed a collective taste for what feels human, without ever explicitly defining what that means.

One answer, of course, is that all of this is temporary. With Opus n+1, we're done.

That might be true. But we've been saying some version of this for three years now, and somehow, we're still here. So maybe instead of trying to predict when we'll become obsolete, we should try to understand why we haven't become obsolete yet.

And for that, I want to borrow an idea from one of the oldest questions in philosophy.

## On Human Consciousness

For centuries, philosophers have tried to answer a seemingly simple question - what is consciousness? What makes us, us?

We've studied the brain, its cells, neurons, even our DNA. Yet we still can't fully explain how consciousness emerges from all these individual parts.

There's a philosophical concept closely related to this called [emergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence) - the idea that something can arise from the interactions of many smaller parts, even though none of those parts has that property on its own.

And there's another interesting thing about us humans. As the philosopher Michael Polanyi put it, "we can know more than we can tell." He was describing something called [tacit knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacit_knowledge) - things we know but can't easily explain.

Much of that comes from experience. A random conversation, something that happened years ago, a thought during a morning run. Most of these things never become explicit memories, but they still change how we think, what we believe, and how we act.

So perhaps understanding a human isn't just about knowing everything they know. It's also about the experiences that made them who they are - and reading about an experience isn't the same as having lived through it.

## We Will (Probably) Still Be Needed

Models already outsmart us in many ways. They have [agency](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1894099637218545984), and they're [developing taste](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement) - at least the kind of taste you can learn from reading everything we've ever written. Both will keep improving. Maybe Opus n+1 will close the gap even further.

But I think there's a difference between knowing everything about something and actually experiencing it.

We're still the ones having random conversations, making mistakes, developing opinions, having fears and desires. All those things shape who we are, and in turn, how we approach problems and what we build.

I keep asking questions because I've shipped a solution to the wrong problem before. I know which problems are genuinely hard because I've been burned by the ones that looked easy. I care about the next two years because I'm the one who'll get paged when it breaks. And the thing I can't put my finger on - the same thing that makes us allergic to slop - is just experience I never consciously stored.

Even making sure my agents aren't blocking other people's agents, the part of my job that feels most like leftovers, runs on this. Knowing who's working on what, which team will care, what broke last time. Most of that was never written down. I just know it, because I was there.

Maybe one day agents will have their own experiences, too. Maybe they'll develop intuitions they can't quite explain, just like we do.

But until then, perhaps our leverage isn't in what we know, but in everything we've experienced that made us who we are.
